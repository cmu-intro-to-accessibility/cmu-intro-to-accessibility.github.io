## Project Context

Many assistive technologies exist to help people with disabilities adapt computer technologies for their access needs. Some of these relate to their senses (e.g., blind or low vision individuals may use a screen reader to access a GUI) and others relate to their motor capabilities (e.g., a person with quadrapelegia may use a sip-and-puff device to control a computer with two switches/buttons). 

Newcomers like you will find these technologies strange and unusual. You will probably become frustrated and angry at how difficult they are to use to accomplish your goals. This is ok because *you are not your user*. As a designer, learning how to empathize with your user is a key skill to help you design for their needs. And, it shows that while it is fairly easy to spot the barriers people with different abilities have when using interfaces designed for non-disabled people, it is a wholly different challenge to figure out how to reduce these barriers.

### 1. Select two accessibility technologies (ATs) and try out their tutorials. 

When selecting your AT, ensure that 

1. The technologies you choose are intermediaries between a disabled user and some website, app or other technology. For example, a screen reader works with any accessibly designed website, or app.
1. One must be a screen reader (there are many that are freely available). The other should be substantially different, i.e., address different accessibility needs. For example, VoiceOver and NVDA are too similar because they are both screen readers. VoiceOver and Adobe's Read Out Loud feature are too similar because the screen reader provides a strict superset of the reader's features. 

Once you select the ATs, find first-person accounts (e.g., YouTube videos) of people using them and watch them.
